# Reading a transcript faithfully

A machine transcript is a lossy recording of a conversation. These are the
habits that keep what you write from it trustworthy months later. Read this
before writing anything from transcript text.

## Speakers

- The people present are the people who **spoke**. A name on an invitation, an
  agenda, or a calendar entry is not evidence of attendance.
- Speaker labels are authoritative for name spelling — they come from the
  meeting platform's roster, not from someone's ear. Teams writes them as
  "Last, First"; render them however the surrounding document does, but do not
  "correct" a spelling against your own guess.
- An unattributed passage stays with whoever was speaking before it. Never
  assign it to someone because the content sounds like them.

## Transcription errors

- Fix garbled technical terms — species names, tool names, acronyms, product
  names — **silently**, when the intended term is unambiguous from context. The
  transcriber mishears jargon constantly, and preserving the mishearing serves
  nobody.
- When a load-bearing term will not resolve, **ask**. A guessed accession
  number, hostname, or gene name is worse than an acknowledged gap.
- Numbers are the least reliable thing in any transcript. Treat a spoken figure
  as approximate unless it was repeated or confirmed in the room.

## What is not content

- The opening run of greetings and the closing sign-off chatter carry no
  content. Neither do audio and connection complaints.
- Repeated false starts, filler, and someone reading a document aloud that you
  already have are not findings.

## What you may not add

- Do not invent links, ticket numbers, or identifiers for resources that were
  mentioned. Plain text only. If someone said "the RT ticket," write that, not
  a URL you constructed.
- Do not supply the detail the conversation left out. A gap in the transcript is
  a gap in the record.

## Evidence, not truth

A transcript is evidence of what was **said**, not of what is true.

- A decision the group reached is a decision. Write it plainly.
- A claim one person made about a system, schedule, or another team's intent is
  that person's claim. Attribute it.
- An inference *you* drew is yours, and needs to be marked as such wherever the
  destination has a convention for that.
- Timestamps are verification anchors rather than content. Cite one when a claim
  is surprising or contested, so a reader can go listen.

## Proportion

Coverage follows substance. Be brief where a topic had little in it and go
deeper where it had more. There is no length target, and padding a thin topic to
match the others misrepresents the meeting.

If the transcript is unclear or incomplete in a way that affects what you are
producing, ask before generating it rather than generating around the gap.

## Where the output goes

This reference covers reading. The shape of what you write is the destination's
business:

- **Formal minutes** — where a recurring meeting has its own minutes workflow,
  that skill owns the structure, filing, and publication.
- **A project wiki** — the `micro-project` skill distills a transcript into
  durable facts in `NOTES.md`, unanswered items under `## Open questions`, and
  work in `TASKS.md`, preserving the transcript under `sources/`. Its
  `YYYY-MM-DD AI:` attribution line is the right home for anything the
  transcript only implies.
- **Anything else** — a summary, a decision log, an answer to one question.
  Match the surrounding document, and keep the rules above regardless.
